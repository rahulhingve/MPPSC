Historical Underpinnings

What This Lecture Covers

This is Section 1 of 4 of the "Historical Underpinnings" chapter (Laxmikanth, Ch. 1). It traces how a trading company became a ruling power, and how the British Parliament slowly began regulating that company:

  1. East India Company's arrival and monopoly charter (1600) and early trade footing (Surat, Jahangir).
  2. Political decline of the Mughals after Nadir Shah's invasion (1739) and the Company's "Divide and Rule" opportunity.
  3. Battle of Plassey (1757) and Battle of Buxar (1764) — from trader to ruler.
  4. Treaty of Allahabad (1765) — Diwani rights over Bengal, Bihar and Odisha (Orissa).
  5. The Company's financial crisis (1770s) and why the British Parliament stepped in.
  6. The Regulating Act, 1773 — first step towards Parliamentary control and administrative centralisation.
  7. The Amending Act / Act of Settlement, 1781 — first attempt at separating executive and judiciary.
  8. Pitt's India Act, 1784 — the System of Double Government (Court of Directors + Board of Control).

Big picture to hold in your head: the British Crown never directly ruled India during this phase — it only progressively tightened its control over the East India Company, while the Company continued to administer India on the ground. That is "Company Raj" (~1757/1773–1858), distinct from the later "Crown Raj" / British Raj (1858–1947).

1600 — The Charter & Monopoly

MUST REMEMBER🔥 PYQ-pattern

The East India Company was not just a trading firm — it was backed by the English state (the Crown), and it was carrying forward England's broader European "imperialism" push (other powers — France, Denmark, Portugal, the Dutch — were doing the same in Asia).

Around 1600, the Company received a trading licence from the English Crown — this licence is called a Charter. Crucially, this charter was given only to the East India Company: an exclusive licence, technically called a monopoly.

The Company then set up a factory at Surat, obtaining goodwill/permission from Mughal Emperor Jahangir to trade — this is the "trader" phase, before the Company turned into a ruler.

🧠 Memory Trick

चार्टर = Exclusive Licence = मोनोपोली। Yaad rakho: "1600 mein Charter mila, aur wo sirf EIC ko mila — isliye Monopoly."

Nadir Shah's Invasion (1739) — the political opening

IMPORTANT

Especially after the Mughals, Nadir Shah's attack (Battle of Karnal, 24 February 1739) broke the "spine" of Mughal power — after this massacre in Delhi, the Mughals never recovered their old strength. This caused political fragmentation across India — regional powers began asserting themselves independently.

The English exploited this fragmentation using their old strategy — Divide and Rule — turning regional rivalries to their advantage.

MUST REMEMBER

By 1757, under Robert Clive's leadership, the Company won the Battle of Plassey — its first real taste of military effectiveness ("we can actually win"). But the truly world-changing victory was the Battle of Buxar (1764), where a combined force of three powers — the Nawab of Bengal (Mir Qasim), the Nawab of Awadh (Shuja-ud-Daulah), and the Mughal Emperor himself (Shah Alam II) — lost decisively to the Company.

Why Buxar matters more than Plassey for exams: Plassey gave the Company its first foothold (via political intrigue/betrayal); Buxar proved outright military supremacy over the era's biggest combined power, and directly led to the Treaty of Allahabad.

Treaty of Allahabad (1765) & Diwani Rights

MUST REMEMBER🔥 PYQ-pattern

Soon after Buxar, the defeated Emperor Shah Alam II sought protection from the Company and signed the Treaty of Allahabad (1765). In exchange for an annual pension, he granted the Company the Diwani rights — the right to collect land revenue and administer civil justice — over three rich provinces: Bengal, Bihar and Odisha (Orissa).

Diwani rights = civil administration + revenue collection (tax) rights. This is different from Nizamat rights = police/criminal-justice/military administration, which nominally stayed with the Nawab's deputy (Naib Diwan/Naib Nazim) for a few more years. This split of Diwani (Company) + Nizamat (Nawab's deputy) from 1765 is what historians call the "Dual Government of Bengal" under Robert Clive — abolished later by Warren Hastings in 1772.

🧠 Memory Trick

दी-वानी में "धन" (Diwani = Dhan/Revenue)। नि-जामत में "न्याय-पुलिस" (Nizamat = Nyay/Police)। Both start differently, so don't mix them — Diwani is money, Nizamat is muscle/justice.

The "Legitimacy" Concept

SUPPORTING

Before Buxar, the Company's money-making in India looked like extortion/plunder to people back in England ("रंगदारी") — the Company's own officers were seen as corrupt looters. After Buxar and the acquisition of Diwani rights, the Company effectively became the tax collector of the region — and tax collection (however harsh) carries social legitimacy that plunder does not.

Analogy used in the lecture: a local strongman ("gunda") demanding money has no legitimacy, even if people pay up — but an official tax notice from the government is paid willingly, because the collector has legitimacy. Buxar is what converted the Company from "gunda" to "government" in the eyes of contemporaries.

This is a conceptual/analytical point — useful for statement-based and "significance of Buxar" questions, not a fact to memorise verbatim.

🎥 Watch: the legitimacy analogy (20:56)

Financial Crisis → Road to Regulation

IMPORTANT

Despite the huge revenue windfall from Diwani rights, the Company reported a massive loss (~£1 million / 10 lakh pounds, circa 1772) to its shareholders. The real reason: large-scale personal enrichment/corruption by Company officers (Robert Clive became one of the richest men of his time), not genuine military expenditure as claimed.

Facing bankruptcy, the Company approached the British Government for a bailout loan. The Government agreed — but on a quid pro quo (Latin: "something for something," give-and-take) basis: in return for the rescue, the British Government demanded the right to regulate/monitor the Company.

Key nuance: The British Government did not take over the rule of India directly. It only took control over the Company; the Company continued to rule India. This is why the period is still called "Company Raj," not "Crown Raj," even after 1773.

Regulating Act, 1773

MUST REMEMBER🔥 PYQ-pattern

Passed by the British Parliament, piloted by Prime Minister Lord North. This was the first law by which the British Parliament began regulating/controlling the East India Company's affairs (also loosely referred to, along with its successors, as one of the "Charter Acts" — see the ⚠️ Don't Confuse note below).

Main provisions

  • MUST Designated the Governor of Bengal as the Governor-General of Bengal. Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General of Bengal.
  • MUST Created a 4-member Executive Council ("Council of Four") to assist the Governor-General — an executive council, not a legislative one.
  • MUST Made the Governors of Bombay and Madras subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal — the first step towards centralisation of governance in India.
  • IMP Established a Supreme Court at Calcutta (Fort William), 1774 — with 1 Chief Justice + 3 other judges. First Chief Justice: Sir Elijah Impey.
  • IMP Prohibited Company servants from private trade and from accepting gifts/bribes.
  • IMP Required the Company's Court of Directors (its governing body in London) to report to the British Government on revenue, civil, and military affairs.

🧠 Memory Trick

One sentence to remember all four pillars: "Supreme Court बना, Council बनी, Bombay-Madras झुके, Private Trade बंद हुआ।"

SUPPORTING

The in-lecture demonstration MCQ (why "Board of Control" is a trap here)

The instructor deliberately tests: "Which of these are features of the Regulating Act, 1773 — (1) Supreme Court at Calcutta, (2) Council for the Governor-General of Bengal, (3) subordinated Bombay & Madras governors, (4) established a Board of Control for political affairs?" The correct answer is only 1, 2 and 3 — the Board of Control did not exist in 1773; it was created eleven years later by the Pitt's India Act of 1784. This exact trap (attaching "Board of Control" to 1773) is a classic exam trick.

LOW PRIORITY / SUPPLEMENTARY

The Regulating Act's vague division of powers between the Supreme Court and the Governor-General-in-Council led to real conflicts — the Raja Nand Kumar case (1775) is the standard textbook illustration of this clash (not detailed in this lecture, but commonly cited alongside the Act's failure). ⚠️ VERIFY exact details in your source before quoting in Mains

Why the Regulating Act, 1773 is considered a failure

It lacked clarity — the boundaries between the powers of the Supreme Court, the Governor-General's Council, and the Court of Directors were not clearly defined. Vague laws are always more exploitable ("जितनी लॉ में क्लेरिटी कम, उतना मिसयूज ज़्यादा") — this ambiguity caused repeated institutional clashes and made the Act largely unworkable, necessitating the Amending Act of 1781.

Amending Act, 1781 (Act of Settlement)

MUST REMEMBER🔥 PYQ-pattern

Also called the Act of Settlement, 1781 — passed to fix the Regulating Act's biggest flaw: the unclear jurisdiction between the Supreme Court and the Governor-General-in-Council. ⚠️ Do not confuse this with the Pitt's India Act of 1784 — they are separate acts, three years apart.

Main provisions

  • MUST Introduced, for the first time in India, the concept of Separation of Powers — by clearly defining what the Supreme Court could and could not do, versus what the Governor-General-in-Council (executive) could do.
  • IMP Restricted the Supreme Court's jurisdiction to Calcutta only.
  • IMP Exempted government servants/revenue collectors/judicial officers acting in their official capacity from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction.
  • IMP Empowered the Governor-General-in-Council to frame rules, ordinances and regulations for provincial courts, and allowed appeals from provincial courts to go to the Governor-General-in-Council (not directly to the Supreme Court).
SUPPORTING — exam phrasing to memorise verbatim

"It represented the first attempt in India to separate the Executive from the Judiciary, by clearly defining their respective jurisdictions." This exact framing is commonly tested.

🧠 Memory Trick

"1781 = अठारह इक्यासी में अलग-अलग" — Judiciary अलग, Executive अलग. The rhyme of the year with "अलग-अलग" (separate-separate) is your cue for "Separation of Powers, 1781."

🎥 Watch: Amending Act introduced (50:42) · Separation of Powers (52:53) · Full feature list (54:54)

Pitt's India Act, 1784

MUST REMEMBER🔥 PYQ-pattern

Named after British Prime Minister William Pitt "the Younger." This Act split the Company's functions into two clearly separate tracks — a system usually called the "System of Double Government."

Commercial affairs

Handled by the Company's own Court of Directors (trade, investment, business decisions).

Political / military / revenue affairs

Handed to a brand-new body appointed by the British Government: the Board of Control.

IMPORTANT

This was the first time the British Government secured supreme control over the Company's civil, military and revenue affairs in India — a bigger leap than the Regulating Act, which had only made the Company's Court of Directors report to Parliament. Even so, the Company continued to administer India on the ground; the Crown still had not taken over direct rule.

This "System of Double Government" (Court of Directors + Board of Control) continued right until 1858, when the Company was abolished after the Revolt of 1857 and Crown Rule began directly.

5. "Regulating Act" vs the term "Charter Act"

The lecture broadly groups 1773 / 1793 / 1813 / 1833 / 1853 together as periodic charter-renewal laws. But in standard nomenclature, only 1773 is called the "Regulating Act"; the later renewals (1793, 1813, 1833, 1853) are specifically called "Charter Acts." Calling 1773 a "Charter Act" is a loose simplification, not the standard exam term. ⚠️ POSSIBLE ERROR in casual usage — use "Regulating Act, 1773" in your answers, not "Charter Act, 1773."
